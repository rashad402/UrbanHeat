How the prediction works

Baseline land surface temperature is measured, not modelled: it is the dry-season median of Landsat 8/9 thermal observations (2015–2026) at 30 m, cloud- and water-masked.

The change you see is produced by a physics-informed neural network. It is trained on the satellite record but constrained by the surface energy balance (Rn = G + H + LE), with latent heat estimated independently by Priestley–Taylor so the constraint genuinely binds.

That constraint is the point. An intervention pushes inputs far outside the observed range — a cool roof sets albedo about 15 standard deviations above the training mean. An unconstrained network trained on the same data predicts that a cool roof warms the surface. The physics term is what keeps extrapolation physically valid.

What the numbers do and don't tell you

Reported ΔT is scaled to realistic coverage — roofs are a fraction of a ward. The share of built surface treated is shown as an adjustable assumption because it multiplies every result; where building footprints have been measured, the per-ward value is used instead of a flat guess.

Two separate uncertainties are reported, and they are not the same thing. The spread (p10–p90) is genuine variation between pixels inside your selection. The model error is the deployed checkpoint's held-out RMSE on absolute temperature; because ΔT is a difference of two predictions from the same model, that figure over-states the error on ΔT. Neither is a confidence interval on a policy outcome.

Why R² is modest

The footer shows a held-out R² of about 0.60, where an ordinary regression on the same data reaches about 0.84. That gap is deliberate. A cool roof pushes albedo far outside anything the satellite record contains, and a model tuned only for accuracy gets that extrapolation wrong — it can predict that a cool roof warms the surface. The deployed checkpoint accepts a lower score in exchange for responses that obey the energy balance.
